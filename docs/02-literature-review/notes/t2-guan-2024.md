## Problem
Modern neural sequence-to-sequence models for post-OCR text correction require large volumes of aligned noisy-to-clean training pairs. Acquiring real-world parallel post-OCR datasets through human crowdsourcing or manual transcription is expensive and time-consuming, especially for low-resource languages. Consequently, researchers rely on synthetic noise generation to corrupt clean text corpora for training. Existing studies rarely compare different synthetic data generation strategies systematically or examine how clean corpus volume and augmentation replication factors affect multilingual neural models across diverse scripts and resource levels.

## Method
The authors conduct a systematic comparative study across three dimensions:

**1. Four Synthetic Data Generation Methods:**
 clean text corpus $A$ is segmented into chunks of up to 230 characters (140 for Russian, 90 for Telugu) and corrupted using one of four methods:
* **Method 1 (Random Injection):** Filters rare characters, picks a chunk target error rate $p \in [0, 15]\%$, and randomly injects character substitutions, deletions, and insertions at a fixed $5:1:1$ ratio (following Jatowt et al., 2019).
* **Method 2 (Image Creation):** Renders clean text chunks into images using supported fonts, applies visual degradations (random rotation, pixel noise, dilation, erosion, and $0\%–50\%$ resolution reduction), and runs an OCR engine on the degraded images.
* **Method 3 (Real-World Error Injection):** Passes a separate in-domain clean corpus $C$ through an OCR engine, aligns the OCR output with ground truth using RETAS to extract character-level confusion probabilities, and injects errors into corpus $A$ according to the learned distribution.
* **Method 4 (Glyph Similarity):** Renders characters across multiple fonts $F$ and computes pairwise visual similarity $S_{\text{norm}}(i, j)$ between characters using computer vision feature detectors $Q = \{\text{ORB, AKAZE, SIFT}\}$ via Jaccard index over average keypoint distance. Based on ICDAR 2017/2019 statistics showing that $1:1$ substitutions represent 87.9% of $1:n$ errors, it models $1:1$ substitutions weighted by visual glyph similarity (alongside random insertions and deletions at $5:1:1$) without requiring aligned real OCR training data.

**2. Data Volume & Augmentation Scaling:**
Evaluates fractions of the original clean dataset ($1/6, 1/3, 2/3, 1$) combined with stochastic augmentation replication factors ($1\times, 2\times, 4\times, 8\times$).

**3. Model Architectures:**
Compares pretrained multilingual subword Transformers (**mT5-base**, **mBART-large-50**), a pretrained byte-level Transformer operating on UTF-8 bytes (**ByT5-base**), character-level models (**CharBERT** with and without visual glyph embeddings), a non-pretrained Transformer trained from scratch (**SCRATCH**), and a conventional voting baseline (**ENSEMBLE**).

## Data
* **Corpora & Languages (8 languages):** Clean text corpora sourced from Project Gutenberg, CC-100, and Kaggle, measured in characters:
  * *High/Moderate-resource:* English (27,883,394 chars, Gutenberg), Spanish (7,813,245 chars, Gutenberg), Russian (6,613,093 chars, Kaggle), German (3,758,352 chars, Gutenberg).
  * *Low-resource / Complex script:* Telugu (5,777,551 chars, Kaggle), Irish (5,090,436 chars, CC-100), Frisian (3,426,499 chars, CC-100), Icelandic (3,147,864 chars, CC-100).
* **Data Splits:** For each language, $20\%$ of the corpus is reserved as the auxiliary corpus $C$ (to extract OCR error distributions for Method 3). The remaining $80\%$ is split $8:1:1$ into training, validation, and test sets.
* **OCR Engine Setup:** Training and validation OCR outputs (for Methods 2 and 3) are generated using the **Google Vision API**, whereas test set OCR outputs are generated by rendering degraded text images and recognizing them with **Tesseract**.

## Evaluation
* **Metrics:** Character Error Rate (CER) and Word Error Rate (WER), plus relative CER reduction (\%) and sentence-level CER change distributions (percentage of test sentences where CER decreased, remained unchanged, or increased after post-processing).

## Key results
**1. Experiment 1 (Data Volume & Augmentation Scaling on English, Icelandic, Russian, Telugu):**
* Increasing original clean data volume and augmentation replication consistently lowers CER. Replicating data from $1\times$ to $4\times$ yields substantial gains (e.g., full-data English mT5 CER drops from $3.42\%$ at $1\times$ to $3.00\%$ at $4\times$, and Telugu drops from $27.70\%$ to $26.31\%$), whereas scaling from $4\times$ to $8\times$ yields diminishing returns ($2.93\%$ on English, $26.32\%$ on Telugu) at double the compute cost (~35 hours on an RTX 4090 for $4\times$ English). Thus, $4\times$ is selected as the optimal trade-off.

**2. Experiment 2 (Synthetic Data Methods & Model Comparison):**
* **Synthetic Methods:** Method 3 (Real-World Injection) and Method 4 (Glyph Similarity) consistently outperform Method 1 (Random) and Method 2 (Image Creation). Method 2 performs worse than Method 3 partly because Google Vision (used in training) makes fewer errors than Tesseract (used in testing), creating a train-test error distribution mismatch. Method 4 matches or beats Method 3 without needing aligned OCR training data.
* **Model Architectures:** Pretrained models (**ByT5**, **mT5**, **mBART**) substantially outperform **SCRATCH** and **ENSEMBLE**. **ByT5-base** and **mT5-base** achieve close performance, with **ByT5** achieving the best overall CER under Method 4 (e.g., English: $4.96\% \rightarrow 3.00\%$; Icelandic: $10.09\% \rightarrow 8.28\%$; Russian: $4.13\% \rightarrow 2.14\%$; Telugu: $34.12\% \rightarrow 25.28\%$). Adding glyph embeddings to CharBERT ($3.54\%$ vs. $3.57\%$ CER on English Method 4) provides no meaningful gain.

**3. Experiment 3 (Cross-Language Evaluation: ByT5-base + Method 4 + $4\times$ Augmentation):**

* While 76.18% of Telugu sentences saw a CER reduction, sentence-level analysis (Figure 5) reveals **overcorrection**: in Irish, **25.37%** of sentences experienced an *increase* in CER after correction (compared to **8.66%** in Russian), attributed to out-of-vocabulary proper nouns in CC-100 and weaker representation of low-resource languages in ByT5 pretraining.

## Strengths
* Systematically isolates the effects of clean data size, stochastic augmentation factor ($1\times–8\times$), synthetic noise strategy, and neural architecture across 8 languages.
* Demonstrates that byte-level modeling (**ByT5**) avoids subword tokenization issues and performs strongly across scripts, and that **Method 4** can synthesize plausible visual confusion noise without requiring parallel real-world OCR data.
* Reports sentence-level degradation rates alongside aggregate CER/WER, exposing overcorrection behavior in low-resource languages.

## Weaknesses
* **Synthetic-to-real & printed-only scope:** All test sets are synthetically degraded printed images recognized by Tesseract; the study does not evaluate on naturally degraded historical documents or human handwriting.
* **Font dependency and $O(n^2)$ scaling in Method 4:** Method 4 relies on printed digital fonts (which do not capture writer-dependent handwriting variation), focuses only on $1:1$ substitutions, and requires $O(n^2)$ pairwise feature comparisons across the character inventory.
* **Train-test engine mismatch:** Using Google Vision for training data generation (Methods 2 & 3) and Tesseract for test evaluation confounds the comparison of Method 2.

## Relevance to our project
* **Candidate neural architecture (ByT5 vs. mT5):** Because ByT5 operates directly on UTF-8 bytes rather than a fixed subword vocabulary, it can process all Ethiopic (Fidel) characters without out-of-vocabulary subword fragmentation. However, since byte sequences are longer than subword sequences and low-resource pretraining coverage varies, we must empirically test whether ByT5 or mT5 performs better on Amharic and meets our app's latency constraints.
* **Synthetic data strategy for Amharic (Method 3 vs. Method 4):** Since paired handwritten Amharic data is limited, we can leverage synthetic data with $4\times$ stochastic augmentation to train our post-correction model. Because Method 4 computes glyph similarity from printed fonts—which may not match how humans write Fidel characters—we have two options to test: (1) using Method 3 by running our T1 Amharic handwriting recognizer on a held-out validation set to extract real empirical Fidel confusion probabilities and injecting those into a larger clean Amharic text corpus, or (2) using printed Fidel glyph similarity (Method 4) as a cold-start fallback if aligned recognizer outputs are too small.
* **Guarding against overcorrection:** The finding that 25.37% of Irish sentences got worse after neural post-processing reinforces that we must evaluate sentence-level degradation and correct-to-wrong rates on Amharic, particularly on proper nouns and rare inflected forms.
