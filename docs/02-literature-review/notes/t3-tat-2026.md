**T3 fields**

- Handwriting or print: printed documents. The authors say legible handwritten dates can be read, but irregular writing fails (Section 4.5).

- Editing and export: the user checks and edits the extracted date, with manual entry as a fallback. Export is not described.
- Free or paid: not stated. The paper describes a research app.
- Tested with a handwritten Amharic sample: no. The paper does not say that the app is publicly available.


## Problem

People miss the expiry date on passports, IDs, licences and prescriptions. Reminder apps need the date typed in by hand, and the authors say this leads people to stop using them. Scanner apps read the text but send the document to a cloud server, which the authors see as a privacy and legal risk for identity and medical documents. The paper wants to automate 
date entry and keep the data private with client-side encryption.

## Method

DocGuard is a Flutter app. Google ML Kit reads the text on the device. On top of that the authors add a rule-based step called LASH (layout-aware sequence heuristic). It joins the OCR lines into one text stream, finds dates with a regular expression, and scores each date by its distance to keywords. 

- A strong keyword (EXP, SKT, TETT) gives 200 minus a distance penalty. A soft keyword (VALID, DATE) gives 100 minus the penalty. An issue or birth keyword (for example ISSUE or DOĞUM) gives −150.
- Keywords are searched within 300 characters. The penalty is 0.3 per character beyond a distance of ten.
- A future date gets +50, and a date within the next ten years gets +30. The highest score wins.
- If confidence is low, the app stops and asks the user to enter the date. The paper does not give the confidence threshold.
- The window (300) and the penalty (0.3) were tuned by grid search on a separate set of 50 images. The other weights and the keyword lists are fixed by hand.

Around that: Firebase login, AES-256-GCM encryption with a key derived by PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA256 (10,000 iterations), a local Hive database, encrypted sync to Firestore, and reminders at 90, 60 and 30 days (Table 2).

## Data

500 document images from 50 templates: 100 ID cards, 100 passports, 150 warranty certificates and 150 medical reports. 200 images came from volunteers (passports, ID cards and driver's licences, with personal details redacted) and 300 came from public sources such as Wikimedia Commons (mainly warranties and medical reports). The authors group them as structured (IDs, passports) and semi-structured or unstructured (warranties, reports).
 
The limitations section says the OCR dictionaries and heuristics are built for Latin-based alphabets, specifically English and Turkish. The paper does not list the languages in the dataset.

## Evaluation

Exact match of the extracted expiry date. Five-fold cross-validation, with folds stratified by document template category, and the 50 tuning images kept out of the test folds. McNemar's test with continuity correction compares the pipelines. Three pipelines are compared: Tesseract + regex, ML Kit + regex ("baseline OCR"), and ML Kit + LASH. The raw ML Kit output is also scored with CER and WER.
 
A separate time-on-task study had 30 volunteers (aged 20 to 45) enter five documents by hand and then with the app (paired-samples t-test). Encryption and OCR timings were measured on a Snapdragon 732G phone with 6 GB RAM (Section 4.2).
 
## Key results
 
| Pipeline | Structured (mean ± SD) | Unstructured (mean ± SD) |
|---|---|---|
| Tesseract + regex | 71.3% ± 1.6% | 48.2% ± 2.4% |
| ML Kit + regex | 84.0% ± 1.0% | 64.0% ± 1.9% |
| ML Kit + LASH | 94.0% ± 1.2% | 82.0% ± 1.9% |
 
Table 3, five folds.
 
- McNemar's test: LASH improved on the regex baseline for structured documents (χ²(1) = 21.34, p < 0.001) and for unstructured documents (χ²(1) = 38.76, p < 0.001) (Section 4.1).
- ML Kit's raw transcription: CER 3.2% and WER 5.4% on structured documents, 6.8% and 9.6% on the others (Table 4). Of the failures that remain, 58.5% come from OCR misreads and 41.5% from the scoring step (Section 4.1). 
- Time per document: 36.5 ± 4.2 s by hand and 10.8 ± 1.9 s with the app. The paper puts the reduction at about 75% (the abstract says 70 to 75%), with t(29) = 34.62, p < 0.001 (Section 4.3).
- Section 4.2: encryption of a 50 KB payload took 12.4 ms (± 2.1 ms) and ML Kit OCR took about 1.18 s.
- Table 7 (memory and speed): Tesseract + regex about 210 to 520 MB and 600 to 1,500 ms; ML Kit + regex about 50 to 130 MB and 120 to 350 ms; the LASH layer under 1 MB, with 1.18 s for the full OCR + LASH pipeline.
- Table 7 also lists LayoutLMv2/v3 (above 94.0%) and a BERT-based model (about 91.0%) on unstructured documents. The paper says LASH is about 12% lower than these heavy models on unstructured data.

## Strengths

- Recent and open access. The mobile architecture and the scoring formula are described in enough detail to follow.
- Separates OCR errors from post-processing errors.
- Sends uncertain results to the user instead of saving them.

## Weaknesses

- The task is finding one expiry date, not reading text, so the accuracy numbers do not carry over to line recognition.
- Latin script only, which the authors say themselves. Handwriting appears only in the limitations, with no result.
- The confidence threshold that sends a result to the user is not given, and the paper does not report how often the manual fallback was needed.
- The usability evidence is time only. Participants always entered the five documents by hand first and then with the app, and entry errors were not measured.
- Table 6 compares commercial scanner and tracker apps, but the paper reports no tests of them.

## Relevance to our project

- **It cannot read Amharic, so it gives us no usable recognizer.** The authors say their OCR dictionaries and heuristics are built for Latin alphabets, English and Turkish. The ML Kit text recognition v2 languages page (https://developers.google.com/ml-kit/vision/text-recognition/v2/languages, accessed 22 Nov 2026) has no Amharic entry among its supported, experimental or mapped languages. We cannot wrap an existing mobile engine and need to train our own recognizer.
- **Sending uncertain results to the user fits our review step.** DocGuard stops and asks for the value when confidence is low. Our proposal already lets users fix the text before saving or exporting, so marking uncertain characters or words could help them check those first. Whether our recognizer's confidence scores are reliable enough for this is untested.
- **Their privacy argument applies to our server-based setup.** The authors avoid uploading identity and medical documents because of the legal and privacy risk. Handwritten notes and letters can be personal too, and our proposal sends images to a remote server and stores them there. We need to decide and state how long images are kept and whether users can delete them. Running recognition on the phone would avoid the upload, but it is a different trade-off that this paper does not settle.
